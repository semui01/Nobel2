The Nobel Prize in Physics is awarded by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. Every year its Nobel Committee for Physics asks for nominations, and every year the letters are sealed.

Nominations stay secret for 50 years, and the public archive leaves out any letter whose nominee is still alive. For physics the archive now runs from 1901, the year of the first prize, to 1975: {{letters}} letters naming {{nominees}} people.

The Swedish thumb

One country counted for more than the rest.

Each year the Academy invites its own members, the members of the Nobel Committees for Physics and Chemistry, and physics professors at Nordic universities to nominate, along with past laureates and professors at universities it picks abroad.

The overlooked

That leaves {{over_n}} who cleared the same bar and never won the physics prize.

Four of them won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry instead: Ernest Rutherford, Irène Joliot-Curie, Frédéric Joliot and Otto Hahn. None of the others won a Nobel Prize at all.

How long they ran

Like many winners, they were nominated year after year. Henri Poincaré drew {{poin_noms}} nominations between 1904 and 1912, the year he died.

Nikolai Bogoliubov's nominations ran from 1959 to the end of the archive in 1975: {{bog_noms}} of them, from {{bog_backers}} people.

Arnold Sommerfeld

No physicist in the archive was nominated more often without winning. Arnold Sommerfeld drew {{som_noms}} nominations from {{som_backers}} people in {{som_countries}} countries between 1917 and 1951, {{som_laureates}} of those people laureates .

He met every condition but one: no nominator in Sweden ever put his name forward.

Several of his students won the prize, among them Werner Heisenberg, Wolfgang Pauli and Hans Bethe. Sommerfeld died in 1951 after being hit by a car in Munich.

Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck

Samuel Goudsmit and George Uhlenbeck were graduate students in Leiden in 1925 when they proposed that the electron spins.

They were nominated together on {{gu_joint}} letters, and each drew {{gu_noms}} nominations from {{gu_backers}} people, {{gu_laureates}} of them laureates. A line joins two nominations made on the same letter.

Like Sommerfeld, they never had a backer in Sweden. Neither won.

Lise Meitner

Lise Meitner was nominated {{mei_noms}} times for the physics prize between 1937 and 1967, and {{mei_chem}} times for chemistry: {{mei_total}} in all.

Her physics letters most often paired her with her nephew Otto Frisch ({{mei_frisch}} letters), with whom she explained nuclear fission in 1939, or with Otto Hahn ({{mei_hahn}}). Hahn alone received the 1944 chemistry prize.

Albert Einstein

Winners waited too. Albert Einstein was first nominated in 1910, by the chemist Wilhelm Ostwald. Between 1910 and 1922, {{ein_backers}} people nominated him {{ein_noms}} times.

The committee held back the 1921 prize and gave it to Einstein in November 1922, for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect rather than for relativity.

The women

Women are rare in this archive: {{women_n}} of the {{nominees}} people nominated for the physics prize. Two of them won it, Marie Curie in 1903 and Maria Goeppert Mayer in 1963. The next woman to win the physics prize was Donna Strickland, in 2018.

Chien-Shiung Wu, whose 1957 experiment showed that nature can tell left from right, was nominated {{wu_noms}} times from 1958 on and never won.

Who was doing the writing

Women were rarer still among the writers: {{wn_n}} of the {{named}} nominators the archive names are recorded as women.

None of their {{wn_noms}} nominations was for a woman. Marie Curie nominated J.J. Thomson and Henri Poincaré.

Methodology and data sources

In short

This analysis covers every nomination for the Nobel Prize in Physics from 1901 to 1975 that the Nobel Prize nomination archive has made public. A nomination is one person putting one physicist forward in one year: a letter with several signers or nominees counts once for each pairing. "Won" means won the physics prize at any time, including after 1975. Nominations sent after a nominee's own prize was announced ({{after_win}}, for {{after_win_people}} laureates) are left out of every rate but kept in totals and the database. A laureate backer is a nominator whose own Nobel Prize, in any category, had been announced before the nomination. A nomination's country is the one it was sent from, under today's borders. Figures reflect the archive as read in October 2026.

Scope

The archive holds {{letters}} physics letters for 1901–1975, which give {{nominations}} nominations: {{nominees}} people nominated and {{nominators}} nominators. Nominations stay secret for 50 years, and by the archive's rules any letter naming a nominee who is still alive is left out entirely, so recent years under-represent physicists who are still living.

Sources

The Nobel Prize nomination archive at nobelprize.org: each year's list for physics, every letter's page and every person's page, read in October 2026. The Nobel Prize's laureate records (api.nobelprize.org) for who won which prize and when each prize was announced. Country outlines from Natural Earth via the world-atlas package.

Cleaning

Names are shown as the archive gives them, without middle initials and with {{name_fixes}} misspellings corrected; laureates are shown by the name in the Nobel Prize's records. Where the archive files one person under two records, they were joined only when the names could not belong to different people: the same first and last name with birth years that agree; two spellings or transliterations of one name with the same birth year (Zavojskij and Zavoisky, Mueller and Müller); two identical sets of initials writing from the same country; or two records that match the same laureate. {{merges}} records were joined this way.

The archive links laureates to their prizes only some of the time, rarely for prizes after 1975. {{linked}} more laureates were found in the Nobel Prize's records by matching name and birth year, so that someone like Frederick Reines, nominated from 1960 and awarded the prize in 1995, counts as a winner.

Where a letter's city contradicts its country (letters from Leiden filed under Germany, for example), the city decides; {{country_fixes}} addresses were corrected this way. Countries are counted by today's borders: letters from the Soviet Union count under Russia or Ukraine by city, and letters from Strasbourg before 1919 count under France. Gender comes from the archive or the Nobel Prize's records, never from a first name; the archive's other gender codes are treated as not recorded. Every change is listed, with its basis, in the corrections file that accompanies the data.

Analysis

Rates compare people, not nominations: "{{b10_hi_pct}}% won" means {{b10_hi_pct}}% of the physicists in that group won the physics prize. Backers are distinct nominators; a letter whose writer the archive withholds ("N.N.", {{anon_letters}} letters from 1962 to 1975) counts as one nominator. A Swedish backer is a nominator writing from Sweden. The busiest tenth are the {{bt_people}} nominators ({{bt_pct_people}}% of all nominators) who wrote {{bt_min}} or more nominations each. The right to nominate (professor at an invited university, Academy member and so on) is recorded consistently only until 1949.

Limitations

The archive is not complete, and it says so. It adds years and corrections over time. Professions and institutions are the archive's own wording and sometimes reflect a person's later posts. Some nominees are institutions rather than people. This is an independent analysis; it is not affiliated with the Nobel Foundation or the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.
